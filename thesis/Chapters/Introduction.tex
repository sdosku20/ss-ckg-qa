\chapter{Introduction} \label{chapter:introduction}
% ============================================================

A hospital knowledge graph and a large language model (LLM) each supply what the
other lacks. The graph holds clinical facts in a form that can be inspected,
corrected and audited one edge at a time, but reaching those facts requires
knowing a query language. The model removes that barrier while offering no
guarantee about what it says. Joining them is appealing for precisely that
reason: neither is a substitute for the other.

Whether the result is any good turns on a step that tends to receive little
attention. Before an LLM can answer anything, something must choose which part of
the graph it is allowed to see. This thesis is about that choice, known as
\emph{subgraph selection}. It studies the choice on a clinical knowledge graph in
daily use at University Hospital Basel and measures one principled strategy
against the system that answers such questions there today.

\section{Motivation} \label{sec:motivation}

The leading method in the general-purpose setting has never been tried in this
one. G-Retriever~\cite{he2024gretriever} casts subgraph selection as a
Prize-Collecting Steiner Tree (PCST) problem, so that relevance and subgraph size
are traded off inside a single optimisation rather than by filtering afterwards,
and it reports large gains on general textual graphs. Those gains were obtained
on small open-domain benchmarks. On WebQSP, a standard set of questions answered
over the general-knowledge graph Freebase, each question arrives with a
pre-extracted subgraph of its own, averaging 1{,}371 nodes. A hospital graph is a
different kind of object, and Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement} sets out the
respects in which it differs, every one of them measured during this project.

Then there is the question of what the comparison is against. SnapQuery is no
research baseline rebuilt for the occasion. It is a deployed service that
clinicians and data managers at the Swiss Transplant Cohort Study (STCS) Data
Center depend on, written by the people who maintain the graph and tuned against
the questions its users actually ask. Outperforming it would mean considerably
more than outperforming a paper baseline; failing to outperform it would be
more informative still.

\section{Problem Statement} \label{sec:problem-statement}

Handing the model the whole graph is out of the question. Serialising a graph
into text is easy enough in principle, but context windows are finite and a
hospital-scale graph produces more text than any current model can read, at a
cost in time and compute that would rule the approach out even if it fitted. The
limit is concrete here. The planner model behind the comparator studied in this
thesis serves a context of 8{,}192 tokens, into which the live database schema
and the agent's own reasoning must also fit. One prepared patient graph, at
45{,}562 edges and something like ten tokens per serialised triple, comes to
roughly $4.5 \times 10^{5}$ tokens. Less than one per cent of a single patient
can be placed in front of that model at once.

Selection is therefore unavoidable, and it can fail in two directions. Retrieve
too little and the facts the answer rests on are simply absent. Retrieve too much
and the result is slow to process, awkward to read, and diluted by material
competing with the relevant part for the model's attention. No strategy escapes
this trade-off; the families of strategies that navigate it are set out in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:background}.

Three properties of the graph studied here sharpen the problem considerably, and
not one of them holds in the benchmarks G-Retriever was evaluated on.

\paragraph{Scale.} The graph holds 21{,}537{,}305 nodes and 72{,}218{,}890 edges
for 1{,}197 patients, roughly 18{,}000 nodes per patient. Graph preparation
reduces this substantially, yet a single prepared patient still comes to
15{,}810 nodes and 45{,}562 edges, about eleven times an average WebQSP sample,
for one patient out of 1{,}197. Selecting over the whole graph for every question
is not merely expensive. It is structurally different from the published setting,
where the candidate region had been fixed in advance and was small.

\paragraph{Sparse and shared text.} Every method compared in this thesis decides
what to retrieve by matching the words of a question against words attached to
each node, so the amount of \emph{distinct} text in a graph limits how finely any
of them can discriminate. This graph holds very little of it. For a node to carry
text of its own would mean a description stored on that node and describing that
particular event; here almost every node is described only indirectly, by
referencing a shared code node that hundreds or thousands of other nodes
reference as well.

Laboratory results are the clearest case, and they account for 20{,}523{,}891 of
the graph's nodes, or 95.3\%. The SPHN schema, the Swiss Personalized Health
Network standard this graph follows, represents one result as four separate event
nodes, none of which holds a description. The words come instead from a
referenced code in LOINC~\cite{loinc}, the international vocabulary for
laboratory tests. Counting distinct descriptions across the cohort showed that
4{,}832{,}744 laboratory tests share just 544 of them between them.

Graph preparation (Chapter~\ref{chapter:methodology}) folds those codes into the
nodes referencing them, which helps without dissolving the effect: one patient's
15{,}810 prepared nodes still carry only 614 distinct descriptions, roughly 26
nodes per description. The consequence for this work is direct. A similarity
score can divide that patient's graph into at most 614 groups and has no
information whatever inside a group. It can identify which \emph{analyte} a
question concerns, meaning which substance was measured, but never which of the
sixty-odd measurements of that analyte is wanted. Every strategy compared here
receives that same signal and therefore inherits the same ceiling, and
Chapter~\ref{chapter:results} reports what happens as a result.

\paragraph{Degenerate connectivity.} A handful of administrative nodes dominate
the structure. There are 168 provenance nodes, which record which hospital system
a record came from rather than anything clinical, of average degree 69{,}525, and
six anatomical-site nodes absorbing 3{,}454{,}881 edges between them. Any two
nodes lie two or three hops apart through one of these, which leaves an
unqualified connectivity requirement close to vacuous.

Independently of the graph, the relevance signal is itself only a proxy. The
signal used throughout is cosine similarity: a \emph{sentence encoder}, a model
trained to turn a piece of text into a vector such that texts with similar
meanings get nearby vectors, is applied to the question and to each node's text,
and the angle between the two vectors becomes the score. That score is not
relevance, and terse clinical labels are not the open-domain prose most sentence
encoders were trained on. A node can rank highly for the wrong reason, or rank
poorly despite being exactly right, because it uses a clinically equivalent but
differently worded term. A selection strategy has to stay useful under an
imperfect signal, not only under a favourable graph.

\section{Research Questions} \label{sec:research-objectives}

The thesis addresses three questions.

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] Does PCST-based subgraph selection retrieve answer entities more
effectively than SnapQuery on the same clinical questions, measured as
\emph{answer-node recall at a given subgraph size}: the fraction of entities
constituting a correct answer that appear in the retrieved subgraph, reported
against the size of that subgraph rather than as a single number?

\item[RQ2] How much of any difference is attributable to the
\emph{connectivity constraint} itself, rather than to the underlying similarity
scores? The constraint is the requirement that the returned subgraph be
connected, meaning every returned node is reachable from every other using only
returned edges. It is what allows a node of no direct relevance to be retrieved
because it lies on a path between two relevant ones, and it is the property that
distinguishes PCST from selecting nodes independently. Internal ablations answer
the question by holding the similarity signal fixed and varying only how
connectivity is handled: similarity-only top-$k$ selection, which need not
return a connected result, and breadth-first expansion from seed nodes, which is
connected by construction.

\item[RQ3] Does augmenting the PCST prize function with \emph{intent} signals
(recognising that a question concerns a treatment, a laboratory value, or a
complication) and \emph{schema} signals (raising the prizes of nodes whose SPHN
type matches that intent) improve the trade-off further?
\end{description}

The working hypothesis for RQ1 is that PCST-based selection achieves higher
answer-node recall at a given subgraph size, with the widest margin on questions
that require several entities to be connected through the graph rather than a
single node to be matched. The reasoning behind it is asymmetric in an
interesting way. SnapQuery returns exactly the rows its generated query names, so
an entity it does not explicitly ask for cannot appear in its result, whereas the
connectivity term in PCST admits bridge nodes and thereby gives indirectly
related answers a chance to surface. RQ3 will be pursued only if the work on RQ1
and RQ2 stays on schedule.

\section{Scope and Delimitations} \label{sec:thesis-scope}

The evaluation runs in two phases. Phase~1, which is the core of the thesis,
measures retrieval quality alone: whether the retrieved subgraph contains the
answer entities, and how large it is, irrespective of whether a downstream model
would phrase a fluent answer from it. Phase~2 measures answer quality, having an
LLM generate a natural-language answer from each retrieved subgraph and assessing
it for faithfulness and hallucination~\cite{huang2023hallucination}. Phase~2
belongs to this thesis rather than to future work.

Query latency is deliberately excluded. It is a property of an implementation and
a deployment rather than of a selection strategy, and including it would reward
engineering effort instead of the retrieval behaviour under study.

The graph is the one held in Neo4j, the graph database SnapQuery itself
queries, scoped to the STCS transplant cohort and structured according to the
SPHN schema~\cite{sphnschema}. That schema is public and versioned. The
instance data is governed clinical data and remains on University Hospital
Basel and BioMedIT infrastructure throughout, so development proceeds against
the schema, against synthetic graphs reproducing its measured structure, and
against the real data once access permits, with final validation on the real
cohort.

\section{Contributions} \label{sec:contributions}

This section states what is new in this thesis and claimed as its own work,
separating that from the published methods it builds on. It serves as a summary
for a reader deciding what to read, and as a checklist against which the
concluding chapter can be held.

Two kinds of item appear. Some are artefacts: an implementation, a procedure, an
evaluation protocol, a question set. Others are \emph{findings}, and it is worth
saying why they belong here rather than only in the results. A property of a
published method that its authors did not report, derived from their own
definitions and then confirmed by measurement, is a result about the method
rather than about this data, and it is the kind of thing another researcher would
want to know before applying G-Retriever to a graph like this one. Each finding
below therefore names both the chapter that derives it and the chapter that tests
it, so that the claim can be checked rather than taken on trust.

\begin{itemize}
\item An implementation of PCST-based subgraph selection for an SPHN-structured
clinical knowledge graph, verified to reproduce the published G-Retriever
implementation exactly on shared inputs, so that any difference in behaviour on
clinical data is attributable to the data and not to the reimplementation.

\item A \emph{graph preparation} procedure that makes an SPHN instance graph
amenable to text-based retrieval at all. It folds shared terminology nodes into
the text of the nodes referencing them, prunes provenance hubs, and scopes
selection to a patient or cohort. Each step is stated as a methodological choice
with a measured justification, and each can be disabled for ablation.

\item Three findings about how G-Retriever transfers to this setting, each
derived from its prize and cost definitions in Chapter~\ref{chapter:pcst} and
then measured in Chapter~\ref{chapter:results}:
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item its edge-prize mechanism collapses when a graph has few, heavily shared
  relation types, because the per-tier prize budget is divided among all
  identically-scored edges. Under the published default parameters this drives
  the effective per-edge cost to a negligible value, and the method returned the
  entire graph it was given;
  \item with text shared at high multiplicity, top-$k$ node selection is decided
  by tie-breaking rather than by similarity, so two implementations of the same
  algorithm return different subgraphs for the same question;
  \item at matched subgraph size the connectivity constraint contributed
  nothing. Every strategy tested recalled the same amount, connected or not,
  because on this graph the similarity signal rather than the structural strategy
  decides what is retrieved.
  \end{enumerate}

\item A terminology cross-walk that resolves codes to canonical descriptions in
five reported tiers, rather than embedding whatever label the graph happens to
carry. On questions asking about a diagnosis that carries only a code, where the
answer is a single node and answer-node recall is therefore either $0$ or $1$ per
question, it moves the mean from $0.00$ to $0.80$: eight of ten such questions
returned their answer node, against none beforehand. The gain is identical for
every retrieval strategy, which places the step in graph preparation rather than
in retrieval, and it reverses entirely when the catalogue's language differs from
the question's.

\item A candidate-generation stage that makes selection applicable at patient
scale at no cost in recall, reported together with the \emph{ceiling} it imposes
so that a narrowing failure cannot be mistaken for a selection failure. It proves
to depend on terminology resolution having been applied first.

\item An evaluation protocol that compares a subgraph retriever against a
production query-generating agent on a shared metric, despite the two exposing
different artefacts, together with an explicit treatment of the questions this
metric cannot score, so that they are reported rather than silently counted as
failures.

\item A human-validated gold set of approximately 50 questions with manually
confirmed answer entities, used to establish correctness independently of
SnapQuery's own output.

\item An assessment of answer faithfulness and hallucination for answers
generated from the retrieved subgraphs (Phase~2), and, as an optional extension,
of the intent- and schema-aware prize function of RQ3.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Structure} \label{sec:thesis-structure}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:background} places PCST-based selection among the
alternatives and introduces the clinical setting.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:pcst} supplies the mathematics left out there and derives
three predictions from the prize and cost definitions.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:methodology} covers graph preparation, the two-stage
retrieval design, the strategies compared and the evaluation protocol, and
Chapter~\ref{chapter:implementation} covers the implementation together with the
evidence for its fidelity to the published one. The measurements, and the tests
of the three predictions, are in Chapter~\ref{chapter:results}. Chapter~7
concludes.
