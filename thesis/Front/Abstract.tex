\chapter{Abstract}

% Revised 29 August 2026. The previous draft described the project as being in its
% second week and stated its results in the future tense. This version reports what
% has been measured and says plainly what has not. The last paragraph must be
% revised again once the SnapQuery comparison and Phase 2 are complete.

Clinical knowledge graphs record medical entities -- diagnoses, drugs, laboratory
results -- together with the relations between them, in a form that can be audited
one fact at a time. That makes them an attractive backend for question answering,
but a large language model placed in front of such a graph cannot be shown all of
it: a hospital-scale graph produces far more text than any model can read at once.
Something must first choose which part of the graph the model sees. This thesis
studies that choice, called subgraph selection, on the Swiss Personalized Health
Network (SPHN) graph of the Swiss Transplant Cohort Study at University Hospital
Basel, and compares the Prize-Collecting Steiner Tree (PCST) formulation
introduced by G-Retriever against SnapQuery, the assistant clinicians and data
managers at the STCS Data Center already use.

The comparison is made on answer-node recall at a given subgraph size: how much of
a correct answer the retrieved subgraph contains, reported against how large that
subgraph is. Two internal ablations -- similarity-only top-$k$ selection and
breadth-first expansion -- separate the contribution of the connectivity
constraint from that of the underlying similarity scores.

The graph turns out to differ from the benchmarks G-Retriever was validated on in
ways that change how the method behaves. It holds 21{,}537{,}305 nodes for 1{,}197
patients; 95.3\% of it is a single reified laboratory construct; a handful of
provenance nodes carry a large fraction of all edges, one of them 15\% on its own;
and almost no node carries text of its own, so that after graph preparation a
single patient's 15{,}810 nodes share only 614 distinct descriptions. Three
consequences are derived from G-Retriever's prize and cost definitions and then
measured. Its edge-prize mechanism dilutes to nothing when relation types are
shared at high multiplicity, and under the published parameters the method returns
the entire graph it is given. Node selection is decided by tie-breaking rather
than by similarity, so similarity identifies which kind of entity a question
concerns but not which instance. And connectivity is nearly free once
high-degree provenance nodes are present, so at matched subgraph size every
strategy tested -- connected or not -- recalls the same amount.

Two further contributions follow from those measurements. Resolving terminology
codes to canonical descriptions, rather than embedding whatever label the graph
happens to carry, moves diagnoses that no wording could retrieve from zero recall
to 0.80; the gain is identical for every retrieval strategy, which places it in
graph preparation rather than in retrieval, and it reverses entirely when the
catalogue's language differs from the question's. A candidate-generation stage
makes the method applicable at patient scale at no cost in recall, and is shown to
depend on terminology resolution having happened first.

At the time of writing the comparison against SnapQuery has not yet been run, and
all retrieval experiments use a deterministic lexical encoder on a synthetic graph
that reproduces the measured structure of one real patient. The results reported
here therefore concern the behaviour of the method on this class of graph rather
than its clinical retrieval quality. This abstract will be revised once the
SnapQuery comparison and the answer-faithfulness evaluation of Phase~2 are
complete.
